1. Structured PSMA PET Reporting (PROMISE & RECIP 1.0)
A. PROMISE Scoring (Lesion Intensity)
When interpreting a PSMA PET scan, raw SUVmax values are highly scanner-dependent. Therefore, molecular imaging scoring systems (like miTNM, E-PSMA, and PROMISE) standardize uptake intensity by comparing the lesion's SUVmax directly against the patient's own physiological reference organs. This determines the pre-test probability of a lesion being a true metastasis versus a benign physiological/pathological mimic.
Score 0 (No Uptake): Uptake is less than the blood pool (measured at the mediastinum/aorta). Interpretation: Not consistent with prostate cancer; likely a benign cyst, scar, or non-PSMA expressing tissue.
Score 1 (Low Uptake): Uptake is greater than or equal to the blood pool, but less than the normal liver. Interpretation: Atypical or low-grade; in the absence of a distinct morphological CT correlate, isolated Score 1 lesions are frequently benign mimics.
Score 2 (Intermediate Uptake): Uptake is greater than or equal to the liver, but less than the parotid gland. Interpretation: Highly suspicious for prostate cancer if located in a typical metastatic pathway.
Score 3 (High Uptake): Uptake is greater than the normal parotid gland. Interpretation: Definitively positive for high-grade prostate cancer (assuming physiological mimics like ganglia/salivary glands are excluded).
Diagnostic Note: For the miTNM staging framework, any lesion scoring ≥ 1 (above blood pool) in a classic anatomical location with a corroborating CT correlate is typically considered positive for metastatic disease.
Therapeutic Note (Radioligand Therapy): To qualify a patient for Lutetium-177 (Lu-177) PSMA radioligand therapy (e.g., per VISION trial criteria), the targeted tumor lesions MUST demonstrate a PROMISE score of ≥ 2 (uptake greater than normal liver). Lesions with uptake below the liver (Score 0 or 1) will not absorb a sufficient ablative dose of radiation, strongly predicting treatment failure.
B. RECIP 1.0 (Response Evaluation Criteria in PSMA PET/CT)
While PROMISE evaluates baseline intensity, RECIP 1.0 is the standardized framework used to evaluate treatment response on follow-up scans (similar to RECIST for CT). It relies on calculating the total PSMA-avid tumor volume (PSMA-TV) and tracking the appearance of new lesions:
RECIP-CR (Complete Response): Disappearance of all PSMA-avid lesions.
RECIP-PR (Partial Response): A decrease in total PSMA-TV by > 30%, with NO new lesions.
RECIP-PD (Progressive Disease): An increase in total PSMA-TV by > 20%, OR the appearance of any new PSMA-avid lesions.
RECIP-SD (Stable Disease): Disease that does not meet the criteria for complete response, partial response, or progressive disease.

2. Lower Leg and Appendicular Skeleton
A. PSMA Avid Lesions
Enchondroma
Clinical: Age Any (usually 20-40, but persists into old age). Commonly found in the short tubular bones of the hands/feet.
CT Features: Densely mineralized with "rings and arcs" (popcorn) calcification in long bones. However, in the hands/feet, they often grow faster and may lack classic rings and arcs.
Differentiation (Malignant Transformation to Chondrosarcoma): Highly suspicious if endosteal scalloping affects > 2/3 of the cortical thickness (deep) or > 2/3 of the longitudinal length, if the lesion is > 5 cm in size, or if there is extraosseous soft tissue extension causing cortical breach.
MRI (Secondary): High vascularity and high T2/STIR signal; useful to confirm lack of true muscle invasion (differentiating tumor mass from peritumoral edema, which follows fascial planes).
Traumatic / Healing Fracture
Clinical: Age Any (>40 highly susceptible to insufficiency/osteoporotic fractures). History of acute injury or chronic repetitive stress (fatigue fracture).
CT Features: Bone discontinuity. Key differentiator is the "Puzzle Sign"—the fractured trabecular and cortical fragments perfectly align and fit together without missing osteolytic bone mass. This perfectly aligned, non-osteolytic fracture is categorized as PSMA-RADS 1 (definitively benign).
Periosteal Reaction: In normal/stress healing, periosteal reaction develops smoothly and continuously over 2-3 months. Symmetrical callus formation. (Atypical femoral fractures from bisphosphonates show thick lateral beak-like calluses).
Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC)
Clinical: Age < 20 (Children/Teens). Extremely rare in patients >40. Often painful, expansile lesion.
CT Features: Multiloculated, eccentric lytic lesion.
MRI (Secondary): Pathognomonic fluid-fluid levels caused by layering of blood products of different densities (serum vs. erythrocytes) within the cystic spaces. Can occur in up to 14% of cases secondary to a Giant Cell Tumor. Malignant Mimic: Telangiectatic Osteosarcoma (TOS) is the primary malignant mimic of ABC.
B. PSMA Non-Avid / Benign Pitfalls
Clinical: Age Any (Developmental). Crucially, they do not appear de novo or grow rapidly in adults. A "new" bone island in a patient >40 is highly suspicious for a metastasis.
Bone Island (Enostosis)
CT Features: Intramedullary, uniform, intense sclerosis. Density must strictly be >1000 Hounsfield Units (HU) (matching cortical bone).
Margins & Shape: Hallmark "feather-like", "spiculated", or "thorny" margins that interdigitate seamlessly with normal trabeculae (failure of osteoclastic resorption). Its long axis always aligns parallel to the mechanical stress lines of the host bone. (Definitively benign / PSMA-RADS 1).
Note: "Giant" bone islands (>2 cm) may show faint metabolic activity due to remodeling (up to 25% of cases), but density rules and feathered margins supersede PET avidity.
MRI (Secondary): Complete signal void on all sequences. Absolutely no surrounding bone marrow edema (no halo sign). Pitfall: The stark magnetic susceptibility boundary between dense compact bone and fatty marrow causes focal signal "blooming" (artifactual enlargement) on gradient-echo (GE) sequences.
Simple Bone Cyst (SBC)
Clinical: Age < 20 (Children/Teens). Extremely rare in patients >40.
CT Features: Central, purely lytic lesion with strict fluid density and thin sclerotic margins.

3. Femur and Intertrochanteric Region
A. PSMA Avid Lesions
Bone Infarct
Clinical: Age Any (Adults >40 common). Associated with corticosteroid use, sickle cell anemia, or trauma. Approximately 55% of cases occur around the knee (distal femur/proximal tibia).
CT Features: Medullary sclerosis with a characteristic serpiginous (snake-like) margin. The center shows a mixed ground-glass/lucent core due to saponification (amorphous calcium soap precipitation). Often has a targetoid central calcification within a fatty background (-65 to -120 HU).
Healing: Heals from the outside in (peripheral rim enhancement and PSMA avidity on the healing borders). Fat inside the infarct is preserved.
Fibrous Dysplasia
Clinical: Age < 30 (Children/Young Adults). Developmental abnormality. Does not appear de novo in adults >40, though existing asymptomatic lesions may persist into old age.
CT Features: Expansive, intramedullary ground-glass matrix (typically 70-130 HU). Surrounded by a thick, well-defined sclerotic "rim sign." Can cause the classic "Shepherd's crook" deformity in the proximal femur due to slow, repetitive microfractures and remodeling.
PET Differentiation: Lesion-to-Background Ratio (LBR) threshold of 2.5 can help differentiate from malignant processes, but CT morphology is diagnostic.
Clinical: Age 40-60. Highly relevant in the prostate cancer demographic.
Liposclerosing Myxofibrous Tumor (LSMFT)
CT Features: Distinct predilection for the intertrochanteric femur (typically 40-50 years old). Has a thick sclerotic rim similar to fibrous dysplasia, but the internal matrix is highly irregular, containing focal areas of macroscopic fat and very low density.
Paget's Disease of Bone
Clinical: Age > 50. Extremely common incidental finding in the prostate cancer demographic. Predilection for axial skeleton (pelvis/spine) and long bones.
CT Features:
Lytic Phase: "Blade of grass" or V-shaped advancing lytic edge in long bones.
Sclerotic/Mixed Phase: Unmistakable increase in bone caliber/expansion (unlike metastases, which destroy but do not expand the whole bone outline). Irregular patches of dense sclerosis, cortical thickening, and wide periosteal reaction.
Spine Key: In vertebrae, Paget's routinely enlarges the pedicles and neural arch ("picture frame" vertebra).
MRI (Secondary): Normal fatty bone marrow is typically spared/preserved, differentiating it from malignant marrow replacement.
Osteosarcoma & Radiation-Induced Sarcomas
Clinical: Bimodal Age (Teens, and >60). Primary osteosarcoma occurs in teens. However, secondary osteosarcomas occur in older adults (>60), frequently arising from malignant transformation of Paget's disease or within fields of prior radiation therapy (e.g., historical pelvic external beam radiation for localized prostate cancer).
Warning: If a highly aggressive, destructive, and intensely PSMA-avid bone lesion suddenly appears strictly localized to a known previous radiation field years later, a radiation-induced secondary sarcoma must be highly suspected alongside a standard prostate metastasis.
CT Features: Aggressive permeative bone destruction, Codman's triangle (elevated periosteum), and "sunburst" or "hair-on-end" spiculated periosteal reaction. Frequently features a large extraosseous soft tissue mass that aggressively destroys cortical boundaries.
Giant Cell Tumor (GCT)
Clinical: Age 20-40. Typical in young adults; less common in the primary prostate cancer demographic (>60).
CT Features: Eccentric, strictly subarticular (reaches the joint surface), osteolytic lesion in the distal femur or proximal tibia. High risk of secondary ABC transformation.

4. Axial Skeleton (Ribs, Spine, Pelvis)
A. PSMA Avid Lesions
Clinical: Age > 50.
Prostate Cancer Bone Metastases
Clinical Spread: Spread is highly correlated to the red marrow distribution (spine, pelvis, proximal femurs) via Batson's venous plexus.
CT Features: Typically present as amorphous, fluffy, or cloud-like sclerotic nodules. They rarely exceed 1000 HU (distinguishing them from bone islands) because the tumor osteoid is disorganized. Sclerotic in ~80% of cases.
Rib Pitfall: A single, isolated linear lytic or sclerotic lesion on a rib is almost always related to trauma, not a solitary metastasis.
MRI (Secondary): Metastases trigger an inflammatory reaction and microfractures, showing a T2/STIR hyperintense "halo sign" (peritumoral edema). Complete marrow replacement occurs (diffuse T1 hypointensity).
Clinical: Age > 50. Classic disease of older adults.
Multiple Myeloma / Plasmacytoma
CT Features: "Punched-out" lytic lesions with absolutely no sclerotic rim. The internal density of the lytic void is intermediate soft-tissue density (30-50 HU), which is distinctly higher than the surrounding fatty marrow (-20 HU).
MRI (Secondary): In a pathologic vertebral collapse, there is uniform, complete T1-hypointense marrow replacement extending into the pedicles. Quantitative DWI shows restricted diffusion (ADC < 1.5 x 10^-3 mm2/s).
Other Malignant Metastases
Breast Cancer: Typically mixed (lytic and sclerotic).
Lung Cancer: Typically purely lytic, highly permeative. Shows a higher propensity to metastasize to the appendicular skeleton.
Kidney (Renal Cell) Cancer: Highly vascular, expansile, purely lytic lesions.
Brown Tumors (Osteitis Fibrosa Cystica / Hyperparathyroidism)
Clinical: Age Any (Common in Adults >40 with end-stage renal disease). Caused by severe hyperparathyroidism.
CT Features: Well-defined, purely lytic, expansile lesions. They can mimic lytic metastases or multiple myeloma, but the key differentiator is the diffuse background of severe osteopenia and pathognomonic subperiosteal bone resorption (most prominent at the radial margins of the middle phalanges and distal clavicles).
PET Differentiation: Underlying parathyroid adenomas may show increased uptake on choline or PSMA PET, leading to the discovery of the disease.
B. PSMA Non-Avid / Benign Pitfalls
Irregular / Regional Osteoporosis (Mimicking Lytic Lesions)
Clinical: Age > 50. Highly common in older adults or post-radiation fields.
CT Features: Severe, uneven demineralization can appear as patchy, moth-eaten osteolysis that visually mimics multiple myeloma or permeative metastases. Key differentiator: There is absolutely no true cortical destruction and no solid extraosseous soft-tissue mass.
MRI (Secondary): Shows patchy marrow edema but lacks a distinct focal, solid T1-hypointense mass completely replacing the marrow (which would be seen in true myeloma or metastasis).
Clinical: Age > 60. Highly prevalent in the prostate cancer demographic.
Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures
CT Features: The "Vacuum Cleft Sign" is the ultimate indicator of a benign, non-malignant collapse. It appears as a horizontal, linear band of gas-attenuation (approximately 92% Nitrogen) directly underneath the depressed superior endplate due to physical distraction forces. Its presence virtually excludes underlying tumor infiltration, categorizing the lesion as PSMA-RADS 1.
MRI (Secondary): The MRI equivalent is the "Fluid Sign" (extracellular fluid replaces the nitrogen when the patient lies supine for the MRI). Marrow edema is band-like and strictly spares the fatty marrow in the non-collapsed regions of the vertebra.
Clinical: Age Any (very common incidental finding in adults >40).
Vertebral Hemangiomas
CT Features: Classic "polka-dot" (axial) or "corduroy" (sagittal) pattern of thickened vertical trabeculae. Crucially, the background matrix between these trabeculae is homogeneously low attenuation (< 20 HU, representing yellow marrow fat), which is highly specific for a benign process and distinguishes it from a sclerotic metastasis.
PSMA Pitfall: Because they are highly vascular lesions, hemangiomas can sometimes display mild to moderate PSMA avidity. This makes recognizing their pathognomonic low-density CT matrix absolutely critical to prevent misclassifying them as true metastases.
Modic Type 1 Degenerative Endplate Changes
Clinical: Age > 40. Extremely common in the prostate cancer demographic with degenerative disc disease.
CT Features: Endplate irregularities, disc space narrowing, and osteophytosis centered on the intervertebral disc boundary (e.g., L4-L5). Can show intense focal PSMA avidity due to active osteoblastic remodeling, mimicking a vertebral metastasis.
MRI (Secondary): Pathognomonic subendplate bone marrow edema (high STIR/T2, low T1) with severe degenerative disc disease—NOT a focal osteoblastic mass.
Diffuse Bone Marrow Activation / Reconversion (Anemia Effect)
Clinical: Age Any. Patients with severe, chronic anemia, those receiving erythropoietin (EPO) therapy, or patients under extreme hematological stress.
PET Features: The aggressively increased erythropoiesis causes diffuse, homogeneous, and highly symmetric PSMA uptake throughout the entire axial red marrow.
Superscan Pitfall: This uniform marrow activation can falsely mimic a malignant skeletal "superscan" (diffuse metastatic spread). The key differentiator is homogeneity: diffuse metastases almost always remain slightly heterogeneous and "patchy" on the MIP projection, whereas anemia-induced marrow activation is perfectly smooth, uniform, and symmetric, with absolutely no corresponding destructive or sclerotic focal changes on CT.
Systemic Symmetrical Osteosclerosis (Metabolic/Hematological Mimics)
Clinical: Age Any. Renal osteodystrophy, myelofibrosis, or mastocytosis.
CT Features: These conditions cause highly uniform, symmetric, and diffuse pelvic and spinal osteosclerosis. Diffuse symmetry is the key differentiator from the heterogeneous, patchy sclerosis of advanced metastatic prostate cancer.
Inflammatory Spondylitis (Ankylosing Spondylitis)
Clinical: Age Any. History of rheumatologic disease.
PET Features: Focal, intensely avid hot spots along the vertebral column or sacroiliac joints. Differentiate by typical inflammatory CT features: syndesmophytes, sacroiliac joint erosions/sclerosis, and bamboo spine appearance.

5. Prostate (Primary TNM Staging)
A. Clinical Pretest Probability
Pretest Probability & PSA Kinetics:
PSA Doubling Time (PSADT): A PSADT of < 6 months is a highly significant predictor for distant macroscopic metastases. Fast kinetics drastically increase the pretest probability of a true-positive finding on PSMA PET, even at lower absolute PSA levels (e.g., 0.2 - 0.5 ng/mL).
Low Pretest Paradox: If the absolute PSA is extremely low (< 0.2 ng/mL) and the PSADT is slow (> 12 months), the statistical probability of finding true disease drops significantly. In these low-pretest patients, an isolated, mildly PSMA-avid lesion is statistically more likely to be a benign false positive (e.g., a ganglion or rib fracture) than a true metastasis.
Biochemical Recurrence Threshold (BCR): Following a radical prostatectomy, BCR is defined as two consecutive PSA values > 0.2 ng/mL. In patients treated with definitive radiation therapy, the clinical trigger for a PSMA PET is any PSA increase > 0.2 ng/mL over the post-treatment nadir. Reaching these thresholds provides a statistically significant pre-test probability of actually locating the recurrent disease on the scan.
PSA Persistence: If the PSA fails to drop and remains > 0.1 ng/mL at 6 weeks post-surgery, this indicates persistent disease (typically residual nodal or micro-metastatic spread) rather than recurrence, and serves as an excellent, strong indication for an early PSMA PET scan.
Other High Risk Factors: Unresected prostate with absolute PSA > 4, PSA Velocity (PSAV) > 2 ng/mL/year, and high ISUP grade (Gleason).
Post-Surgical Pitfall: In the prostate bed after definitive radiotherapy or surgery, postoperative tissue healing and focal inflammation can cause mild PSMA avidity, mimicking local recurrence.
Location Influence: Posterior prostate tumors predictably spread via Batson's venous plexus directly to the axial skeleton. Anterior tumors have a higher propensity for rapid hematogenous spread to visceral organs (liver, lungs).
The Imaging Paradox: Castration therapy temporarily shrinks physical tumor volume but causes the amount of PSMA receptors per surviving cell to surge. Early PET scans may show paradoxically intense uptake that does not accurately correspond to tumor volume.
B. The miTNM Staging Framework
The molecular imaging TNM (miTNM) system standardizes the reporting of disease extent specifically for PSMA PET/CT:
miT (Primary Tumor / Local Recurrence):
miT0: No evidence of primary tumor (negative scan or previously resected without local recurrence).
miT2: Prostate-confined tumor (no extracapsular extension).
miT3a: Extraprostatic extension (tumor extends through the prostatic capsule).
miT3b: Tumor invasion into the seminal vesicles.
miT4: Tumor invasion into adjacent organs (e.g., bladder, rectum, external sphincter, levator ani, or pelvic side wall).
miTr: Local tumor recurrence within the prostate bed (after radical prostatectomy or definitive radiotherapy).
miN (Regional Lymph Nodes):
miN0: No regional lymph node metastasis.
miN1: Metastasis in regional pelvic lymph nodes (nodes below the aortic bifurcation, including obturator, internal/external iliac, and sacral chains).
miM (Distant Metastasis):
miM0: No distant metastasis.
miM1a: Extra-regional lymph node metastasis (e.g., para-aortic, retroperitoneal, mediastinal, or supraclavicular nodes).
miM1b: Bone metastasis.
miM1c: Visceral organ metastasis (e.g., lung, liver, brain).
C. Local Staging & Interpretation Rules
Primary Lesion Scoring:
SUVmax > 12: Strongly suspicious for Primary Tumor (Primary 5). Caveat: Granulomatous infections (e.g., prior BCG treatment for bladder cancer) can cause extreme SUV > 12. However, BCG-induced granulomatous prostatitis typically presents as diffuse avidity throughout the gland, distinguishing it from focal prostate cancer.
Peripheral Zone (PZ): Focal uptake in the PZ is highly suspicious (Primary 4). The PZ comprises the apex (bottom 6mm) and the posterior/lateral aspects.
Central Zone (CZ): Uptake here is often physiological if it is symmetric and diffuse (Primary 2).
Transition Zone (TZ): Focal uptake > 2x the background TZ activity is suspicious (Primary 3). False Positive Pitfall: While BPH is usually diffuse, focal hypermetabolic BPH nodules can act as a prominent benign mimic of TZ malignancy, reducing specificity.
T3a (Extracapsular Extension):
The tumor contacts the prostate pseudocapsule over a broad length of 10 to 12 mm.
Focal fat stranding or spiculation in the periprostatic fat.
Asymmetric bulging, concavity, or convexity causing a localized "desmoplastic reaction" on the prostate contour.
T3b (Seminal Vesicle Invasion):
Loss of the normal "triangular fat plane" separating the prostate base from the seminal vesicles.
Asymmetric seminal vesicles with continuous high radiotracer activity extending upward from the prostate.
CT Density of the seminal vesicle fluid increases to > 40 HU (normal fluid is < 20 HU) due to cellular tumor infiltration.
T3b Pitfall – Senile Amyloidosis: Common in elderly men. Causes intense, symmetric, diffuse PSMA uptake in both seminal vesicles. Differentiated from true SVI because malignant invasion is asymmetric and continuous with the primary tumor.
T3b Pitfall – Urinary Reflux (Post-TURP/HoLEP): In patients with prior transurethral prostate surgery, excreted radiotracer in urine can reflux into seminal vesicles, perfectly mimicking SVI. Resolve with: (1) delayed post-void imaging, (2) CT urography to match contrast pooling, or (3) concurrent bone scan (99mTc-MDP is renally excreted but NOT PSMA-specific—if it pools identically, it's urine).
T4 (Direct Organ Infiltration):
Urinary Bladder: The bladder wall loses its normal smooth/striated appearance and becomes irregular or nodular. Pro-tip: Review under wide bone windows to visually subtract the intense radiotracer accumulation of normal excreted urine.
Rectum: Complete obliteration of the fat plane in the rectoprostatic angle with nodular soft tissue extending into the rectal wall.

6. Pelvis and Abdomen (False Positives & Mimics)
Lymph Node Assessment Criteria
The Thoracic Duct / Supraclavicular Highway (M1a): Prostate cancer lymphatic spread generally moves sequentially up the pelvic and retroperitoneal chains. However, tumor cells can travel directly up the thoracic duct, which empties into the left subclavian vein. Consequently, the left supraclavicular lymph node (Virchow's node) can be a site of early distant (M1a) lymphatic spread. Do not dismiss an avid supraclavicular node simply because the patient lacks massive lower abdominal nodal disease (it may be a "skip" metastasis).
Size Thresholds (Short Axis) & PSMA-RADS Scoring:
Oval/reniform nodes: suspicious if ≥ 10 mm.
Round/spherical nodes: suspicious if ≥ 8 mm.
Prostate-specific optimized threshold: > 6 mm (97% specificity).
Warning: Up to 80% of metastasis-involved pelvic nodes are < 8 mm. ~47% of missed metastases are anatomically normal-sized.
PSMA-RADS 3A (Equivocal Soft Tissue): If a lymph node in a typical drainage pathway demonstrates mild-to-moderate PSMA uptake but is below the established CT size threshold (e.g., a 3-4 mm node with focal avidity), it is classically categorized as PSMA-RADS 3A. It is too small to definitively call malignant (PSMA-RADS 4/5) based on CT morphology alone, but too avid to completely dismiss as a benign reactive node or ganglion (PSMA-RADS 1/2). These require close follow-up or correlation with clinical risk factors.
Morphological Criteria:
Loss of fatty hilum: Normal nodes have a fatty hilum visible on CT. Loss of hilum + round shape = highly suspicious.
Shape change: Benign nodes are oval/reniform; metastatic nodes become round/spherical.
Drainage pathway: Prostate cancer follows predictable pathways—obturator → external/internal iliac → common iliac → retroperitoneal. Avid nodes outside this chain suggest a non-prostate primary.
Ganglia vs. nodes: Ganglia are band-shaped (71%), mean 4mm short-axis, bilateral/symmetric, SUVmax ~1.73. Metastatic nodes are round/nodular (99%), intense avidity.
When staging lymph nodes in the pelvis and abdomen, strictly verify continuity and morphology on non-contrast CT to rule out these common PSMA-avid mimics:
Vascular Mimics: Iliac vessels, pararectal varices, and unopacified veins. Always scroll sequentially to prove the structure is a continuous vessel, not a round/oval node.
Neural Mimics (Ganglia & Schwannomas):
Trigeminal Ganglion (Gasserian Ganglion): Located at the skull base in the middle cranial fossa. Heuristic on CT: Look for a symmetric, soft-tissue density structure sitting in Meckel's cave (a small depression exactly at the apex of the petrous temporal bone), just posterolateral to the cavernous sinus. Because of its physiologic PSMA uptake, it frequently mimics a destructive skull base metastasis.
Stellate Ganglion: Located in the lower neck / cervicothoracic junction (C7-T1 level). Heuristic: Look at the level of the thyroid gland.
Celiac Ganglia: Located in the upper abdomen, flanking the origin of the celiac trunk (T12-L1 level). Heuristic: Look at the level of the upper kidneys.
Hypogastric Ganglia: Located in the lower abdomen / pelvis. Heuristic: Look at the level of the iliac wings.
Sacral/Presacral Ganglia: Located deep in the pelvis, bilaterally along the anterior surface of the sacrum. Heuristic: Look in the presacral space.
Ganglia General Features: Sympathetic and other neural ganglia typically exhibit relatively low SUVmax and are frequently bilateral/symmetric. They are distinguished from lymph nodes by their distinct comma, band, or cigar shape, their characteristically low unenhanced CT density, and a short-axis diameter that is usually < 4 mm (though exceptions occur). They are much longer and thinner than a typical pathologic round node.
Schwannomas: Benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors that can exhibit moderate-to-intense PSMA uptake, closely mimicking malignant lymphadenopathy or soft-tissue metastases. Distinguish them on cross-sectional imaging by their location along major neural foramina/pathways (e.g., presacral space, sciatic nerve) and their typical well-circumscribed, sometimes cystic appearance.
Bowel Diverticula & Diverticulitis: Outpouchings in the large intestine (most commonly the sigmoid colon). When actively inflamed (diverticulitis), they demonstrate focal PSMA avidity due to regional hyperperfusion and macrophage infiltration. Always scroll sequentially to confirm the focal uptake corresponds to an outpouching that communicates with the bowel lumen and contains gas/feces, rather than a true adjacent iliac lymph node.
Splenules (Accessory Spleens): Can be intensely PSMA avid (physiologic). Typically located near the splenic hilum, completely smooth. Must exactly match the unenhanced CT density and morphological texture of the main spleen. Often prominent in patients with a history of left upper quadrant trauma. (Also typically SSTR avid).
Primary Kidney (Renal Cell) Carcinoma: While PSMA-targeted imaging is highly sensitive for distant RCC metastases, the primary kidney tumor is severely masked by the organ's normal physiological background excretion of the tracer. Requires careful correlation with cross-sectional morphology.
The Neoangiogenesis Principle (Other Cancers): PSMA (Prostate-Specific Membrane Antigen) is actually a misnomer; it is highly expressed in the neovasculature (new blood vessel formation) of almost all solid tumors. Therefore, most other cancers (e.g., gastric, hepatocellular, gynecological, pancreatic) can sometimes demonstrate active PSMA uptake, mimicking prostate metastases. Always suspect a second primary if lesions fall outside typical prostate drainage pathways or if the PSA is too low for the observed tumor burden.
Synchronous Colorectal Cancer: One of the most common incidental second primaries found on PSMA PET. Colorectal tumors show PSMA avidity via neovascular endothelial expression (SUVmax often < 5). Key clue: circumferential bowel wall thickening or a discrete colon-based mass on CT, and avid abdominal lesions outside the classic prostate drainage pathway (obturator → iliac chain). A low PSA with a high abdominal tumor burden is a major red flag for a non-prostate primary.
Neuroendocrine Tumors (NETs): While typically imaged with SSTR-PET (DOTATATE), NETs can paradoxically show moderate to intense PSMA avidity due to tumor neoangiogenesis. They typically present as highly vascular masses in the pancreas, bowel, or liver.
Malignant Melanoma: Melanoma metastases (skin, soft tissue, lungs, liver, brain) frequently express PSMA within their tumor-associated neovasculature. They can perfectly mimic widespread, highly avid prostate cancer metastases.
Lymphoma: Aggressive subtypes (e.g., Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma, Follicular Lymphoma) can demonstrate prominent PSMA uptake. Key differentiators are massive, bulky lymphadenopathy (often conglomerate) that spans non-prostate territories (axillary, supraclavicular, mediastinal) combined with hepatosplenomegaly.
Inguinal Hernia Mesh: Foreign-body inflammatory reactions cause mild, diffuse PSMA uptake along the mesh. If the mesh retracts over time, it forms a hyperattenuating pseudonodular pattern mimicking a soft-tissue metastasis.
Adrenal Gland Mimics:
Adrenal Adenoma: Common incidental finding. Benign adrenal adenomas typically show < 10 HU on unenhanced CT (lipid-rich). If < 10 HU, it is definitively benign regardless of mild PSMA uptake.
Pheochromocytoma: A rare neuroendocrine tumor of the adrenal medulla that can demonstrate highly intense, false-positive PSMA avidity (due to abundant neovasculature). Distinguishable from a prostate metastasis by its typical large size, heterogeneous solid/cystic CT appearance, high unenhanced density (> 10 HU), and typical clinical presentation (hypertension, catecholamine excess).
Gallbladder Stones (Cholelithiasis): Gallstones can occasionally demonstrate false-positive PSMA avidity. Ensure focal uptake in the gallbladder fossa correlates with a dense calcified stone or sludge on the non-contrast CT, rather than a true hepatic or nodal metastasis.
2.5 cm Size Threshold (VISION Trial): In advanced theranostic settings, any lymph node with a short axis of > 2.5 cm that demonstrates PSMA-avidity lower than the background liver parenchyma is considered an exclusionary, PSMA-negative metastatic deposit.

7. Head, Neck, and Chest (False Positives)
Physiological Uptake: Salivary glands, lacrimal glands, and nasal mucosa display extremely high baseline PSMA avidity. (The physiological uptake in these regions is typically surpassed only by the renal cortex). Additionally, the pancreas routinely demonstrates variable and heterogeneous physiological PSMA uptake, which can occasionally appear focal and mimic a pancreatic metastasis or primary neuroendocrine tumor if not correlated carefully with the underlying CT parenchyma.
Tracer Nuance: F-1007 PSMA exhibits higher physiological retention in the parotid glands than Ga-68 PSMA. Conversely, F-1007 clears hepatobiliary, meaning minimal urinary excretion, eliminating the intense radioactive urine pooling in the ureters/bladder that frequently masks small adjacent pelvic nodes on standard Ga-68 scans!
Esophageal & Bronchogenic Cysts/Diverticula: Fluid-filled or air-filled outpouchings. Esophageal diverticula can pool swallowed radiotracer or become actively inflamed, perfectly mimicking an avid mediastinal lymph node. Always scroll to confirm continuity with the esophageal lumen or bronchial tree.
Sarcoidosis, Pneumoconiosis & Interstitial Lung Disease: Sarcoidosis classically presents with symmetric, bilateral hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy accompanied by a micronodular pulmonary pattern. Because PSMA accumulates in activated macrophages, these sarcoid granulomas can demonstrate intensely false-positive PSMA avidity, perfectly mimicking massive mediastinal lymph node spread. Occupational lung diseases (pneumoconiosis, such as asbestosis or silicosis), other interstitial lung diseases, and general pulmonary fibrotic changes also routinely show focal or regional false-positive uptake due to chronic inflammation and structural remodeling.
Esophagitis (GERD) & Swallowed Saliva: Benign esophageal inflammation (such as severe reflux esophagitis) directly causes mild-to-moderate PSMA uptake within the esophageal wall itself. Additionally, because the radiotracer is highly concentrated in saliva, natural swallowing causes transient, physiological tracer pooling down the digestive tract (specifically the oral cavity/mouth, larynx, esophagus, and stomach).
Soft Tissue & Chest Wall Pitfalls:
Desmoid Tumors (Aggressive Fibromatosis): Locally aggressive fibroblastic proliferations that can be intensely PSMA-avid (SUVmax up to 12.7), overlapping directly with malignant values. Locations: Most classically arise in the anterior abdominal wall (often at prior surgical scars), intra-abdominally (mesentery), or extra-abdominally in the shoulder girdle or thigh. CT Characteristics: They appear as bulky, poorly circumscribed, infiltrative soft-tissue masses that are iso- or hyperdense to surrounding skeletal muscle and lack calcification. They can perfectly mimic a soft-tissue sarcoma or an aggressive metastasis; biopsy is often required if clinically ambiguous.
Nodular Fasciitis: A benign, rapidly growing fibroblastic proliferation that can occur in the subcutaneous tissues or fascia. It can show intense PSMA avidity due to hypervascularity and myofibroblastic activity, visually mimicking a soft-tissue sarcoma or solid metastasis.
Lipomas & Variant Subtypes: Simple lipomas are definitively benign if they measure uniform fat density (-50 to -100 HU). However, highly vascular subtypes like Angiolipomas or brown-fat tumors like Hibernomas can demonstrate significant PSMA uptake. Always correlate focal uptake in adipose tissue with the unenhanced CT density.
Intramuscular Myxoma: A benign, slow-growing soft tissue tumor classically presenting as a well-circumscribed, ovoid intramuscular mass (often in the thigh, shoulder, or buttocks). Because of its mucoid matrix and neovasculature, it can demonstrate mild-to-moderate PSMA avidity mimicking a soft tissue metastasis or sarcoma. On CT, it typically appears homogeneously hypodense (lower density than adjacent muscle, but higher than fat).
Dermatofibroma, Acrochordon (Skin Tag) & Cutaneous Lesions: Common, benign fibrous skin nodules (dermatofibromas) and pedunculated fibroepithelial polyps (acrochordons) can occasionally exhibit focal PSMA uptake due to fibroblastic/histiocytic proliferation and neovascularity. When encountering a focal hot spot in the superficial soft tissues, carefully evaluate the non-contrast CT to confirm it is completely confined to the dermal/cutaneous layer.
Gynecomastia & Pseudoangiomatous Stromal Hyperplasia (PASH): Benign proliferations of male breast tissue (highly associated with Androgen Deprivation Therapy). Gynecomastia is typically bilateral and symmetric. PASH is a specific localized overgrowth of myofibroblastic stroma within the breast tissue. The proliferating stroma in both conditions can demonstrate intense, highly focal physiological PSMA uptake. This perfectly mimics a chest wall or soft-tissue breast metastasis if its classic subareolar, superficial location within the breast fat pad is not carefully recognized on the non-contrast CT.
Elastofibroma Dorsi: A benign, slow-growing fibroelastic pseudotumor classically located in the subscapular region (deep to the lower pole of the scapula, between the scapula and the posterior chest wall). It frequently shows mild-to-moderate PSMA avidity. Its highly specific anatomical location and its alternating striated pattern of fat and fibrous tissue (which has a CT density closely matching the surrounding skeletal muscles) definitively distinguish it from a malignant sarcoma or chest wall metastasis. It is also frequently bilateral.
Thyroid & Parathyroid Neoplasms: Both benign adenomas (thyroid and parathyroid) and malignancies (e.g., Radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancers) frequently upregulate PSMA expression due to their rich vascularity. An intensely avid focal nodule in the thyroid bed should be correlated with ultrasound; it is highly likely to be a synchronous primary or a benign adenoma rather than a prostate metastasis.
Thymomas: Tumors of the thymus gland (located in the anterior mediastinum) can exhibit significant PSMA uptake. Any avid, well-circumscribed anterior mediastinal mass should be evaluated for thymoma or thymic hyperplasia before assuming it is a metastatic node.
Active Vascular Calcifications: Benign aortic or arterial calcifications can show intensely hypermetabolic foci with SUVmax up to 19.5 on F-PSMA-1007, mimicking metastatic lymphadenopathy.
Meningioma: The most common intracranial PSMA false positive. Distinguished on CT by coarse internal calcifications and its broad-based attachment to the skull with a characteristic "dural tail."
Cerebral Infarction (Stroke): Acute or subacute ischemic strokes can demonstrate significant PSMA uptake due to blood-brain barrier breakdown, neovascularization, and local neuroinflammation. If a patient presents with a focal avid brain lesion, correlate with recent neurologic history and non-contrast CT to rule out a healing infarct before diagnosing a brain metastasis.

B. Additional Thoracic Mimics (Granulomatous, Inflammatory, & Structural)
General Rule for Granulomatous Disease: In the lungs, granulomatous diseases (like TB and Sarcoidosis) frequently demonstrate a strong predilection for the upper lobes. Additionally, they are frequently accompanied by calcifications in the adjacent hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes (e.g., "eggshell" calcifications). The presence of coarse nodal calcification is a key distinguishing CT feature favoring benign granulomatous inflammation over true prostate cancer metastases.
Tuberculosis (TB): Active and reactivated TB can present with prominent, intensely PSMA-avid parenchymal consolidations, cavitary lesions, or nodules in the upper lobes. Crucially, TB can also manifest as massive PSMA-avid lymphadenopathy or destructive bone lesions (Pott's disease in the spine), which are visually indistinguishable from aggressive prostate cancer metastases without clinical correlation. TB sequelae (e.g., retractile bronchiectasis with calcifications) can show mild, persistent tracer avidity.
Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA & EGPA): GPA displays moderate-to-intense focal PSMA uptake in granulomatous lesions. Eosinophilic GPA (EGPA) causes intense, diffuse bilateral lung uptake and ground-glass areas due to active eosinophilic interstitial pneumopathy.
Occupational Pneumoconioses:
Berylliosis (Chronic Beryllium Lung Disease): Exhibits exceptionally high radiotracer uptake with fibrotic and nodular changes (SUVmax up to 21.2).
Anthracosilicosis/Anthracosis: Routinely demonstrates moderate-to-intense PSMA accumulation within mediastinal lymph nodes and fibrotic lesions.
Non-Granulomatous Infections & Inflammatory Opacities:
Pneumonia / COVID-19 / Histoplasmosis: Manifest as mildly to moderately PSMA-avid consolidations or irregular nodules.
Ground-Glass Surrounded Nodules: Single pulmonary nodules with a ground-glass halo typically exhibit low-grade PSMA uptake due to active benign inflammation.
Atelectasis: Laminar or subsegmental atelectasis can display mild, non-specific PSMA uptake.
Bronchiectasis & Middle Lobe Syndrome: Show mild-to-moderate tracer avidity due to chronic localized bronchial inflammation.
Structural Pitfalls:
Bronchogenic Cyst: Contains peribronchial glands lined by cuboidal epithelium. Because these glands share similarities with salivary gland epithelium, they physiologically accumulate PSMA radioligands, mimicking a solid metastasis.
Tracheal Mucus Plug: Benign accumulation of fluid-density mucus within the tracheal/bronchial lumen can demonstrate focal PSMA avidity (SUVmax up to 9.2). Easily identified because it disappears on repeat scanning once the patient clears the airway.
Synchronous Primary Lung Malignancies:
Primary lung adenocarcinomas, squamous cell carcinoma, and SCLC can present as highly PSMA-avid solitary lesions.
Differentiating Feature: True hematogenous prostate metastases are typically multiple, solid, rounded nodules accompanied by extensive pelvic lymphadenopathy or bone metastases. A highly avid solitary lung mass in a patient with undetectable/stable PSA strongly suggests a synchronous primary lung cancer, not a metastasis.
8. Artifacts and Specific Pitfalls
Halo Artifacts (Reconstruction Artifact): Adjacent to organs with intense, saturating physiological uptake (most notably the bladder and the kidneys), the PET reconstruction algorithm can erroneously generate a false "halo" ring of elevated activity or a photopenic (cold) shadow. This can either falsely mimic a lesion immediately next to the bladder/kidney, or conversely, mask a true adjacent lymph node metastasis. Always rely heavily on the unenhanced CT morphology when evaluating tissue immediately bordering these hot organs.
Motion & Misregistration Artifacts: Because the CT is acquired in seconds (often during breath-hold) while the PET is acquired over minutes (free-breathing), significant anatomical misregistration can occur. The most common site is the diaphragm: a true metastasis in the liver dome may falsely project into the right lung base on the fused images, or vice versa. Always check the separate, unfused PET and CT series to confirm true anatomical location if a lesion sits at a respiratory boundary.
Post-Surgical Wounds & Healing Scars: The active fibroblastic repair and neoangiogenesis associated with healing surgical incisions (e.g., recent laparotomy, prostatectomy bed, or sternotomy) frequently upregulate PSMA expression. This causes linear or focal mild-to-moderate false-positive uptake along the surgical bed. Activity typically subsides as the scar matures (usually after 6-12 months).
Radioactive Urine Reflux (SV-Sparing Prostatectomy): In patients who undergo a seminal vesicle-sparing radical prostatectomy (performed in highly selected patients to maximize nerve preservation and functional outcomes), the preserved seminal vesicles can anatomically communicate with the urinary tract. Renally excreted radioactive urine can reflux retrogradely into these remnant seminal vesicles, causing intense, focal, false-positive pooling that perfectly mimics a local tumor recurrence.
Undetectable PSA (< 0.05 ng/mL) False Positives: Attempting PSMA PET with a truly undetectable PSA carries a massive misinterpretation risk. Over 80% of such patients exhibit non-physiological radiotracer uptake (SUVmax < 7), typically as non-specific bone marrow spots in the ribs and pelvis.
Endoprosthesis / Beam Hardening: Hip replacements or dental amalgams cause photon starvation and beam hardening artifacts on CT, which can artificially distort the corresponding PET attenuation correction map, leading to false hot or cold spots.
FOV Edge Artifacts: The first two and last two slices of the axial FOV frequently contain truncation artifacts; lesions here should be interpreted with extreme caution.
Hot-Clot Artifact: Faulty injection technique causes radiotracer to agglutinate with erythrocytes. These microemboli lodge in the pulmonary capillary bed, creating focal, extremely high-activity lung "hot spots" on PET with absolutely no corresponding structural lesion on CT. Crucially, because this is an injection artifact, it will completely vanish on any subsequent follow-up scan.
Radiolysis / Free 68Ga-Citrate: Borderline low pH during radiopharmaceutical preparation releases free 68Ga-citrate, causing abnormally high, persistent blood pool activity in the aorta, IVC, carotid, and iliac vessels—mimicking diffuse lymphadenopathy. Identified by checking the whole-body MIP for intense vascular activity in heart chambers and great vessels.
Flare Phenomenon (Post-ADT): Within the first 2-4 weeks of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), PSMA expression per tumor cell temporarily surges (up to 707% increase in SUVmax documented). "New" lesions appear that were previously invisible. This is a molecular flare, NOT progression. Scanning should be delayed until > 12 weeks after starting ADT for accurate response assessment.
Motion / Respiratory Misregistration: Patient movement or breathing causes spatial mismatch between CT and PET slices, particularly near the diaphragm and bladder. Can create false hot/cold spots at organ boundaries.
Rib "Pearls on a String" Sign: Multiple consecutive rib fractures (traumatic) present as linear, sequential focal hot spots on whole-body MIP. Easily confused with multifocal rib metastases. CT bone windows reveal distinct cortical fracture lines and callus formation.
Osteoid Osteoma: Age 10-25 (Children/Young Adults). Very rare >40. Small intracortical nidus (< 1.5 cm) surrounded by immense, dense cortical thickening. Clinically presents as severe nighttime pain that responds dramatically to NSAIDs. PSMA uptake is highly variable (can be intensely avid or completely negative).
